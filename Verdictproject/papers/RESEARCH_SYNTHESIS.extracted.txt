Scope and reading note.
This document consolidates every formulation, algorithm,
experimental result, and stated limitation from the six supplied papers that bears on the
Calibrated Abstention Agent project. It is organized by the research tracks defined in the
project brief.
Formulas are transcribed from the source materials. Where a paper’s result does not sur-
vive contact with the project architecture, this is stated explicitly rather than presented
as support. Sections marked with a flag identify direct conflicts between a cited paper
and the current design; these are the highest-value content in the document and should
not be skimmed.

The baseline-comparison result to replicate
The authors compared SGR against the natural naive baseline: set the threshold to the value
maximizing coverage while keeping train error smaller than 𝑟∗.
Over 1000 random train/test splits, the naive baseline violated 𝑟∗on the test set
in over 50% of cases, with a mean violation of 18% relative to the requested
𝑟∗.
This is the strongest available argument for why threshold-by-eyeball is insufficient in a reg-
ulated setting: a concrete, citable failure rate against the exact procedure a reasonable engi-
neer would otherwise adopt by default.
Open problems flagged by the authors
Both map onto project gaps and are quoted in substance from the source’s concluding re-
marks:
• Joint training: “the most challenging open question would be to simultaneously train both
the classifier 𝑓and the selection function 𝑔to optimize coverage for a given risk level.”
• Loss generality: “In this paper we only studied selective classification under the 0/1 loss.
It would be of great importance to extend our techniques to other loss functions and
specifically to regression, and to fully control false-positive and false-negative rates.”
Direct relevance flag. SGR assumes 0/1 loss and i.i.d. sampling from a fixed 𝑃. The
project’s extraction fields are numeric (regression-like), and the entire Track 2 premise
is that exchangeability fails. SGR as published does not directly transfer.
What does transfer: (a) the selective-risk definition with its conditional denominator; (b)
the 𝛿/𝑘union-bound discipline; (c) the Gascuel–Caraux bound as the tight primitive rather
than Hoeffding; and (d) the 50%-violation baseline result as motivation.
The authors
themselves name regression extension and FP/FN control as open — meaning the project
cannot cite this paper as having solved the numeric case.

Negative results the design must account for
1.
Failure of confidence-score methods (Section 5.1). The natural pipeline — train a
classifier minimizing misclassification with the target, obtain confidence scores; separately
learn a model predicting whether the expert agrees with the label and extract its confidence;
defer to whichever is more confident (Raghu et al. 2019a) — is a consistent estimator for
both rejector and classifier. It nonetheless fails under limited hypothesis class or limited data
because it does not allow ℎto adapt to the expert’s strengths and weaknesses.
Their toy example: two subpopulations 𝐴∈{0, 1} with 𝑃(𝐴= 1) ≥𝑃(𝐴= 0). The expert
perfectly classifies group 𝐴= 1, which contains cluster (1) separable only by a complex
nonlinear boundary and cluster (2) separable only via expert side-information 𝑍unavailable
from 𝑋. Restricting ℎand 𝑟to 𝑑-dimensional hyperplanes: learning ℎfirst yields a hyperplane
minimizing average error across both groups and separating neither. The optimal behavior is
for ℎto fit group 𝐴= 0 while 𝑟separates the groups. Cluster (2) cannot be separated even
with infinite data — the expert’s side information is required, and the hard task is identifying
that region.

The fairness result — direct Track 7 implications
Section 6.5, hate speech and offensive language detection (Davidson et al. 2017, 24,783
tweets). Discrimination is defined as the difference in false-positive rates between AAE and
non-AAE tweets. With a fair expert (equal error rates across groups, 𝑝= 𝑞= 0.9):
• Classifier alone: discrimination 0.226
• Fair expert alone: discrimination 0.03
• Composed system via confidence-score baseline: discrimination 0.449
The composed system roughly doubled the classifier’s discrimination.
Conversely, with an
expert biased against non-AAE tweets (discrimination 0.084), the confidence method’s dis-
crimination reduced to 0.151. The authors’ framing: this “reiterates the established fact that
fairness does not compose (Dwork & Ilvento, 2018). In fact, the end-to-end system can be less
discriminatory even if the individual components are more discriminatory.”
This is the single most important transferable finding for Track 7. The project
routes to human reviewers. A defensible fair-lending posture cannot be established by
showing the model is fair and the reviewers are fair. Disparate impact must be measured
end-to-end on the composed system, and the composition can be strictly worse than
either component in isolation.
Note the direction of the effect is not even predictable from component properties: a
fair expert made things worse, a biased expert made things better. No component-wise
argument will survive scrutiny. Build end-to-end disparate-impact measurement into the

Second validation (Section 6) — election night
CQR applied to approximately 𝑇= 3000 county-level vote totals for the 2020 US presidential
election, replicating the method the Washington Post used on election night (Cherian & Bron-
ner 2020).
Covariates 𝑋𝑡: county demographics by ethnicity, age, sex, median household
income, education. Counties ordered by time zone (eastern first, Hawaiian last), randomized
within zone, reproducing the observed east-to-west reporting bias.
The key observation: “If the order in which vote counts completed was uniformly random
{(𝑋𝑡, 𝑌𝑡)} would be an exchangeable sequence on which we could run standard conformal
inference methods. In reality, larger urban counties tend to report results later than smaller
rural counties… Thus, the distribution of (𝑋𝑡, 𝑌𝑡) can be viewed as drifting throughout election
night.”
Result: the non-adaptive method fails to maintain 90% coverage, incurring large troughs dur-
ing time zone changes; the adaptive method maintains approximate 90% coverage across all
time points with Bernoulli-comparable deviations.
Transferable insight, and the cleanest analogy available. The drift here arises purely
from non-random arrival order of otherwise exchangeable-looking units — no adversary,
no concept drift, no model degradation. Loan applications arrive in non-random order too:
seasonality, industry clustering, broker channel batching, marketing campaign timing.
This is the strongest argument for why exchangeability fails in the project’s pipeline with-
out needing to posit anything exotic. It is more persuasive to a skeptical reviewer than
the volatility example, because it requires no assumption about market regimes.

The overconfidence mechanism (Section 5.3)
MaxProb values are generally lower for OOD examples than in-domain examples, consistent

are still too high. Calibration plots show MaxProb is underconfident in-domain and overcon-
fident out-of-domain.
Concretely, from their Figure 3b: at MaxProb = 0.6, the model is about 80% likely to
be correct if the question came from SQuAD (in-domain), and about 45% likely to be
correct if it was OOD. When in-domain and OOD examples are mixed at test time, MaxProb
therefore does not abstain enough on the OOD examples. The calibrator shows a smaller in-
domain/OOD gap, indicating improved calibration, despite not being trained on any unknown
OOD data.

Error analysis (Section 5.6) — reusable methodology
Protocol: for two (𝑞known, 𝑞unk) pairs (one similar, one dissimilar), sample 100 overconfident
errors (system confidently gave a wrong answer) and 100 underconfident errors (system ab-
stained but would have been correct), drawn from the 1000 most extreme of each.
Findings worth carrying over:
• NewsQA →TriviaQA: 62% of overconfidence errors were valid alternate answers
or span mismatches — the model predicts a slightly different span than gold and should
be considered correct. “thus the calibrator was not truly overconfident. This points to the
need to improve QA evaluation metrics (Chen et al., 2019).” 45% of underconfidence er-
rors involved coreference over long distances; another 25% were cases with insufficient
evidence in the paragraph (TriviaQA was built by distant supervision), so low confidence
was correct.
• NewsQA →HotpotQA: 34% of overconfidence errors from alternate answers or span
mismatch. On 65% of underconfidence errors the correct answer was the only plausible
span, suggesting the model guessed via dataset artifacts — so “the calibrator’s lack of
confidence is therefore justifiable.”
Build this into the evaluation before reporting any numbers. A substantial fraction
of apparent ”extraction errors” will be format or normalization mismatches — 1,234.00
versus 1234, fiscal versus calendar period labels, gross versus net presentation, parenthe-
sized negatives — that a human reviewer would call correct.
If these are not separated out, the project will systematically understate calibration qual-
ity and overstate error rates. In this paper the effect was 62% of the worst overconfidence
errors on one dataset pair. That is not a rounding artifact; it is most of the error mass.

The central insight
Each LM output defines an uncertainty set via entailment, so correctness is equivalent
to coverage.
Conformal prediction then defines a back-off algorithm that makes outputs
progressively less specific until they are correct with high probability.
The authors’ framing of why prior conformal-for-LLM work stalled: scoring the entire output
space is intractable, and the resulting confidence sets are so large as to be unusable. Defin-
ing conformal sets implicitly via entailment relations sidesteps both — uncountably large un-
certainty sets are never instantiated, and the guarantees land directly on model outputs in
interpretable form.

Their Table 1 example: evaluating 𝑖5 + 𝑖−25 + 𝑖45. GPT-4 computed two of the three expres-

removed the incorrect claim, leaving a partial proof presented to the user with a
single remaining step.
Table 5 shows the pattern more broadly. For a treeks/squigs/goolees word problem, the con-
formal output retains the variable definitions, both derived equations, and the substitution —
then stops before the final incorrect arithmetic, ending at “This equation represents the rela-
tionship between the weights of treeks, squigs, and goolees.” For correct derivations (vertical
asymptotes, distance formula, polynomial addition) the full chain is retained.
This is genuinely the target pattern — abstain on the specific derivation node that is
unreliable, hand the reviewer a partially completed derivation rather than rejecting the
whole case. But be precise about what it demonstrates.
The sub-claims here are reasoning steps in a self-contained math problem with a known
ground-truth answer. The entailment oracle checks each step against 𝑦∗independently.
There is no propagation: the framework does not model how uncertainty in step 1’s output
inflates uncertainty in step 3.
The project’s derived metrics are computed from extracted values whose own uncertainty
propagates — field confidence →derived-metric confidence →case-decision confidence.
Hierarchical uncertainty propagation is not solved by this paper and is not solved
by any paper in this set.
That is the genuine novel contribution if delivered. It is also the component most likely to
be wrong. Flag it as such in the writeup rather than implying the MATH result covers it.

Limitations stated by the authors — read as the threat model
• Coverage is not conditional. “we do not have a guarantee on the conformal set associ-
ated with every input, but instead, we have coverage on average over inputs. This means
that when the calibration set is defined across multiple tasks or users, the factuality of
any one domain or user may differ significantly from the target factuality level.”
• Marginal over the calibration draw. In settings with a small, fixed calibration dataset
whose threshold is reused, realized coverage may deviate.
They quantify it: beyond
the marginal guarantee, requiring high-probability guarantees over the calibration set
reveals additional variation in empirical factuality with standard deviation ≈0.09,
expected to shrink to zero as the calibration set grows.
• Shift breaks the threshold. “in real-world scenarios where distributions change, the
threshold computed on past calibration data can fail to maintain the desired coverage.”
The first limitation is a fair-lending problem, not merely a statistical one.
Marginal coverage across the calibration set means a 90% factuality target could realize
as 95% for restaurant loans and 78% for construction loans. Worse, it could differ system-
atically across segments correlated with protected classes — geography, business age,
industry composition all correlate with race and national origin in SMB lending.
Under ECOA / Regulation B, ”90% on average across the portfolio” is not obviously a
defensible answer when a segment sits at 78%. Combine this with the Mozannar fairness-
does-not-compose result and the exposure compounds.
The brief cites Gibbs, Cherian & Candès, ”Conformal prediction with conditional
guarantees” (2024) as a Track 3 supporting reference. That citation is doing far more
work than the brief credits. Promote it to a core dependency: conditional coverage
is likely a requirement here, not an enhancement. Note the 𝜎≈0.09 figure is the au-
thors’ own measurement of how far realized factuality wanders — roughly nine percent-
age points — which is not a margin that survives a fair-lending review.

Their closing, quoted: “our work is the first step to enabling the application of many sophisti-

The Track 2 + Track 3 composition — ACI’s online update wrapped around con-

Beneish’s derivation of the relevant investor cost ratio: the typical manipulator loses approx-

Honest performance summary
Holdout sample: the model correctly identifies 37.5% to 56.1% of manipulators while incor-
rectly classifying 3.5% to 9.1% of non-manipulators, across the relevant cost range. Esti-
mation sample: 58–76% of manipulators correctly identified, 7.6–17.5% of non-manipulators
incorrectly flagged.
The model is cost-effective relative to a naive all-clean strategy when 𝐶𝐼> 𝐶𝐼𝐼. But, in the

large error rates in the range of error costs that are likely to be of most relevance to investors”

Robustness checks performed: no Pearson correlation among the 36 pairs exceeded 0.25;
dropping DEPI, LVGI, SGAI, TATA one at a time and in combination gave similar results; six
different prior probabilities tested; 100 random estimation/holdout resamples (50 manipula-
